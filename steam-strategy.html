TL;DR

There is no “trailer-only vs trailer+demo” choice for Next Fest. Next Fest requires a publicly playable demo to participate. A trailer-only Steam page is a valid path, but it skips the single biggest wishlist event of the year for indies.

The data is also clearer than people think: a median Next Fest demo only adds about 800 wishlists during the fest. The breakouts that get 10k+ are the games that walked in with strong pre-fest momentum already. Next Fest amplifies momentum, it does not create it.

For our team (3 people, 6 months runway, co-op chaos game where streamers are a natural fit) the recommended path is:

  1. Steam page live by Jun 1, 2026 (already on the schedule).
  2. Killer cinematic trailer (mix of in-engine + AI-assisted, the way we've been working on the props/gameplay shots).
  3. A tight demo — one chaotic mid-flight scenario, 10–15 minutes of play — for Next Fest.
  4. Use the months between June and October to build wishlist momentum before the fest. The fest itself is a multiplier, not a starting point.

Bottom line. Trailer alone is cheaper but leaves the Next Fest boost on the table. A tight demo is more work but unlocks the only large discovery event we have between now and launch. The math favors building a tight demo, not skipping it.

The reality check

Steam Next Fest eligibility, from Valve's own documentation:

  • Game must have a publicly playable demo by the time the fest begins.
  • Each game can participate only once — we pick one Next Fest, ever.
  • Demo submitted for review at least 2 weeks before the fest (4 weeks if we want it live for the press preview).
  • No restriction on demo length, but it must be a publicly accessible build.

So “trailer instead of demo” is not a Next Fest option. It's a choice between (A) launching a Steam page with a trailer and skipping the fest entirely, or (B) building a demo and being in the fest. Mixing both is not on the menu.

How wishlists actually work

Where wishlists come from

Wishlists are the only signal Steam's algorithm uses to give an unreleased game free visibility. They come from four places:

  • Discovery on Steam itself — Coming Soon lists, similar games, the Discovery Queue, tags. Steam will surface us more aggressively if our wishlist velocity (per day) is rising.
  • Steam events — Next Fest, Steam Indie Days, genre-specific fests. Next Fest is by far the biggest.
  • Off-Steam marketing — streamers, social, press, our portfolio site. Anything that gets people to type the game into Steam search.
  • Direct from the trailer — a great trailer that goes mildly viral on Reddit/Twitter/TikTok can do enormous volume on its own.

The numbers from recent Next Fests

From third-party tracking (Ziva, Alinea) of February 2026 Next Fest, sample of 208+ games:

PercentileWishlists added during the fest
50th (median)~806
70th~1,839
95th~13,461
Single top breakout57,074

Sobering fact. Half of all games at Next Fest get under a thousand wishlists during the event. The story of “Next Fest will save our launch” only happens for the top 5–10%.

What predicts a strong Next Fest

The single strongest signal in the 2026 data is a Spearman correlation of r = 0.825 between pre-fest wishlist count and wishlists earned during the fest. Translated: Next Fest mostly amplifies a game that already has momentum. A game with 0 pre-fest wishlists adds a median of ~322. A game with 100,000 pre-fest wishlists adds a median of ~12,882.

The other striking number: 68–88% of wishlists during the fest come from people who never opened the demo. The demo is the ticket of entry into the fest's visibility machinery, but the Steam page (capsules, trailer, screenshots, description, tags) does the actual converting.

Wishlist conversion at launch

Average indie wishlist-to-purchase conversion at launch is somewhere between 5% and 20%, depending on price and genre. So:

  • 10,000 wishlists at launch → roughly 500–2,000 day-one sales.
  • 50,000 wishlists at launch → roughly 2,500–10,000 day-one sales.

These are not life-changing numbers for a three-person studio, but they're enough to validate a sequel/expansion strategy and to get streamer attention, which compounds.

The Steam page anatomy

Even before we decide demo vs no demo, this is the minimum we have to ship by June 1 to have a Steam page live at all:

  • Capsule images at four sizes (small, main, header, library/hero). Steam reviewers reject pages with weak capsules. This is the single most important art deliverable.
  • Screenshots — minimum 5, ideal 8–12. They show up in the Discovery Queue and in the carousel. Our gameplay-mock shots from the last few sessions are a strong starting point.
  • At least one trailer. Steam allows multiple. The first one auto-plays on the page.
  • About this game — 200–400 words, the short version of the pitch.
  • Tags — up to 20. These drive ~30% of Steam discovery. We want “Co-op”, “Comedy”, “Multiplayer”, “Chaotic”, etc. Get these right and the page rides discovery on its own.
  • System requirements and a planned release date or window (we can say “Coming 2026”).
  • Review by Valve takes 3–5 business days the first time.

Optional but worth it: a franchise / publisher page on Steam, so we can cross-promote any follow-up titles or DLC; Steam Community Hub moderation set up before launch.

Path A — Trailer-only, skip Next Fest

What it looks like

Steam page goes live June 1 with a strong cinematic trailer, capsules, screenshots, tags. No playable build. We market the trailer hard between June and October. We launch the full game some time in 2027 without ever having done Next Fest.

Pros

  • Drastically cheaper: one trailer plus some screenshots vs a full demo build.
  • Lower risk: a bad demo can actively hurt wishlist conversion. A great trailer with no demo is pure upside.
  • The team can spend all dev time on the actual game.
  • If our trailer is genuinely great, it can hit the same wishlist numbers as a top-quartile Next Fest demo, purely from social virality.

Cons

  • We lose Next Fest entirely. It is the single biggest one-week wishlist event of the year and we can only do it once.
  • No demo means no streamer playthroughs, which is the natural marketing vector for a co-op chaos game.
  • Steam algorithm bias: games with demos get more aggressive surfacing in the “Recommended for you” queue.
  • No real player feedback before launch — the design is essentially unvalidated until release day.

Rough cost

Trailer + capsules + screenshots: 4–6 weeks of one artist plus some Jorge time for capture. Maybe US$0–5k for any extra rendering/AI tooling. Negligible compared to the full game.

Path B — Trailer + tight demo for Next Fest

What it looks like

Steam page live June 1 with the same trailer/capsules as Path A. A small demo — one chaotic flight scenario, 10–15 minutes — submitted to Steam by early October. Public for the duration of Next Fest, then we can choose to keep it live or take it down.

Pros

  • We get into Next Fest. Even a median outcome is ~800 wishlists in one week, which is more than most months of organic marketing.
  • Demo unlocks streamer/content-creator coverage — for a co-op chaos game, this is the biggest single multiplier we can pull.
  • Real playtest data — we learn what's fun before we lock the final scope.
  • Wishlist conversion at launch is typically higher for games that had a Next Fest demo — players who liked the demo are pre-sold.
  • If our demo is genuinely fun, the breakout outcome (10k+ wishlists) is a real possibility for a co-op chaos game. Lethal Company, Among Us, Content Warning all rode this exact vector.

Cons

  • Real dev cost: a polished slice means 3–4 months of focused work for our two engineers.
  • If the demo is broken or boring, it hurts more than no demo. First impressions on Steam are sticky.
  • Demo content has to be representative of the final game — we can't show stuff we're not actually going to ship.
  • One-shot opportunity: we only get one Next Fest. If we burn it too early, we don't get a second chance.

Rough cost

A “one chaotic flight” demo: 3–4 months of focused work for Valentín + Federico, plus production from Jorge. In actual money this is mostly opportunity cost — the same hours could go into the full game. Expect US$5–15k of out-of-pocket if we need any external help (sound design, QA, etc).

Path C — Steam Playtest first, public demo later

Worth flagging because it's a real third option: Valve also offers Steam Playtest, which is essentially a private demo. We can gate it (sign-up required, key-based access, limited slots), gather feedback from real players, and iterate without exposing rough edges to the whole Steam audience.

Pros

  • Lower-stakes validation. Bad first impressions don't get a public Steam review or land on YouTube.
  • Useful as a bridge: build a Playtest in Aug, use it to fix the worst problems, promote it to a public demo for Next Fest in October.
  • Gives us a list of engaged playtesters who'll wishlist and promote on launch.

Cons

  • Doesn't replace the Next Fest demo — still need a public demo for the fest.
  • Extra production overhead on top of Path B.
  • Smaller eyeballs — private means few people actually see it.

Best used in combination with Path B, not as a substitute.

Recommendation for our team

Path B, with two opinionated constraints.

Constraint 1: keep the demo tight

Do not try to make a full first-act demo. One chaotic flight, three or four mechanics live (serve coffee, fix one thing, defend against one threat, land badly), 10–15 minutes of play, replayable for co-op chaos value. That's it.

The reason isn't ambition — it's that the data says 68–88% of wishlists come from non-demo-players. The demo doesn't need to convert players, it just needs to exist so we qualify for the fest, and be fun enough that streamers don't bounce in the first five minutes.

Constraint 2: build wishlist momentum before the fest

The most important number in our entire campaign is our wishlist count on the morning Next Fest opens. The r=0.825 correlation between pre-fest wishlists and fest performance means the four months between Steam page launch (Jun 1) and Next Fest (Oct 19) are where this gets won or lost.

Specifically:

  • Cinematic trailer up on day one. This is the page's main conversion engine.
  • Submit to as many smaller events as we qualify for between June and October: LudoNarraCon, Day of the Devs, indie genre-specific showcases. Each one is a small wishlist bump.
  • Hand-pick 5–10 streamers who play co-op chaos games. Send them a Steam Playtest key in August or early September. Don't beg, just give them something genuinely fun to play.
  • Devlog content (Twitter/Bluesky/Reddit r/IndieDev) twice a week from June onwards. Cheap, repeatable, compounds.

What this means for the schedule

The existing schedule already targets Jun 1 for the Steam page and Oct 19–26 for Next Fest. That's the right plan. The non-obvious addition is treating August as the “internal alpha + Steam Playtest” milestone — gated, low stakes, used to harden the demo before the public eats it.

Risks worth naming

  • Demo eats the full game. Scope creep on the demo delays the full game and burns runway. The 10–15 minute hard cap on demo content exists to prevent this.
  • Trailer underperforms. The trailer is the single biggest wishlist driver. If it's flat, the rest of the strategy is propping up nothing. Mitigation: make and test 2–3 trailer cuts before launch.
  • We blow our one Next Fest shot too early. Once we participate, we can't again. If the game's not ready, we wait. Better to slip to February 2027 Next Fest than burn it on a broken demo.
  • Multiplayer matchmaking breaks at scale. Co-op chaos games live or die on a smooth first-five-minutes experience. Playtest in August has to specifically stress-test the matchmaking path.
